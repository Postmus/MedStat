Medical Statistics - Answers lab 5

Introduction

This answer page gives one complete set of answers for lab 5. The same numerical results should be obtained when the analyses are carried out in R or SPSS.

The analyses use the Cleveland heart disease dataset and address the following research questions:

  • Does maximum heart rate achieved differ across chest pain types?
  • Is the presence of heart disease associated with sex?
  • Is the presence of heart disease associated with fasting blood sugar?

Exercise 1: Download and open the dataset

The dataset contains 303 patients and 14 variables.

library(haven)

# Convert labelled categorical variables to factors.

heart_data[labelled_vars] <- lapply(heart_data[labelled_vars], haven::as_factor)

Exercise 3: Analyze maximum heart rate across chest pain types

Research question: Does maximum heart rate achieved differ across chest pain types?

The outcome is thalach, and the grouping variable is cp.

Descriptive results

boxplot(
  thalach ~ cp,
  data = heart_data,
  xlab = "Chest pain type",
  ylab = "Maximum heart rate achieved",
  las = 2
)
for (group in levels(heart_data$cp)) {
  group_data <- heart_data$thalach[heart_data$cp == group]
  n <- length(group_data)
  mean_thalach <- mean(group_data)
  se_thalach <- sd(group_data) / sqrt(n)
  ci_thalach <- mean_thalach + c(-1, 1) * qt(0.975, df = n - 1) * se_thalach

  cat(
    group,
    ": n =", n,
    ", mean =", round(mean_thalach, 1),
    ", 95% CI = [", round(ci_thalach[1], 1), ", ", round(ci_thalach[2], 1), "]\n",
    sep = ""
  )
}
Typical angina: n =23, mean =156, 95% CI = [146.9, 165]
Atypical angina: n =50, mean =162.4, 95% CI = [157, 167.8]
Non-anginal pain: n =86, mean =155.4, 95% CI = [151.3, 159.5]
Asymptomatic: n =144, mean =140.7, 95% CI = [136.9, 144.5]

The mean maximum heart rate was lowest in the asymptomatic chest pain group.

One-way ANOVA

anova_model <- aov(thalach ~ cp, data = heart_data)
summary(anova_model)

The one-way ANOVA is statistically significant, \(F(3, 299) = 17.41\), \(p < 0.001\). This indicates that maximum heart rate achieved differs across chest pain types.

The histograms and Q-Q plot can be used to assess whether the distributions are roughly compatible with ANOVA. There is no need to base this decision on a formal test of normality; with these sample sizes such tests are often not helpful for deciding which analysis to use. The group standard deviations are of a similar order of magnitude, and Levene’s test does not show evidence against the equal variances assumption.

Post-hoc comparisons

pairwise.t.test(

  heart_data$cp,
  p.adjust.method = "bonferroni",
  pool.sd = TRUE
)

Using Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise t-tests, the asymptomatic chest pain group has a significantly lower maximum heart rate than the typical angina group, the atypical angina group, and the non-anginal pain group. The other pairwise comparisons are not statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment.

Exercise 4: Analyze heart disease by sex

Research question: Is the presence of heart disease associated with sex?

Note

For the 2x2 tables in this lab, chisq.test(..., correct = FALSE) is used to report the ordinary Pearson chi-square test. This corresponds to the Pearson Chi-Square row in SPSS. If correct = FALSE is omitted, R applies Yates’ continuity correction by default for 2x2 tables; SPSS reports this separately as Continuity Correction.

sex_table <- table(heart_data$sex, heart_data$target)
sex_table
        
         Heart Disease No Heart Disease
  Female            25               72
  Male             114               92
round(prop.table(sex_table, margin = 1) * 100, 1)
        
         Heart Disease No Heart Disease
  Female          25.8             74.2
  Male            55.3             44.7

The row percentages show that the prevalence of heart disease was higher in males than in females.

The expected cell counts are all larger than 5, so the chi-square test is appropriate.

sex_chisq <- chisq.test(sex_table, correct = FALSE)
sex_chisq$expected
        
         Heart Disease No Heart Disease
  Female      44.49835         52.50165
  Male        94.50165        111.49835
sex_chisq

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  sex_table
X-squared = 23.218, df = 1, p-value = 1.446e-06

There is strong evidence of an association between sex and heart disease, \(\chi^2(1) = 23.22\), \(p < 0.001\). The prevalence of heart disease was higher in males (55.3%) than in females (25.8%).

Exercise 5: Analyze heart disease by fasting blood sugar

Research question: Is the presence of heart disease associated with fasting blood sugar?

fbs_table <- table(heart_data$fbs, heart_data$target)
fbs_table
       
        Heart Disease No Heart Disease
  False           117              141
  True             22               23
round(prop.table(fbs_table, margin = 1) * 100, 1)
       
        Heart Disease No Heart Disease
  False          45.3             54.7
  True           48.9             51.1

The row percentages show similar heart disease prevalence in the two fasting blood sugar groups.

The expected cell counts are all larger than 5, so the chi-square test is appropriate.

fbs_chisq <- chisq.test(fbs_table, correct = FALSE)
fbs_chisq$expected
       
        Heart Disease No Heart Disease
  False     118.35644        139.64356
  True       20.64356         24.35644
fbs_chisq

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  fbs_table
X-squared = 0.19339, df = 1, p-value = 0.6601

There is no evidence of an association between fasting blood sugar and heart disease, \(\chi^2(1) = 0.19\), \(p = 0.660\). The prevalence of heart disease was similar for patients with fasting blood sugar at or below 120 mg/dl (45.3%) and patients with fasting blood sugar above 120 mg/dl (48.9%).
